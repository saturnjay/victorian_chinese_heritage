Stories · turning points

History is still unfolding.

Moments of migration, discrimination, resistance, work, worship, family and remembrance connect nineteenth-century Victoria to the community of today.

A connected chronology

Six stories that reshaped Victoria.

1851–59Gold and migration
New Gold Mountain

Thousands arrive, over sea and overland

Gold brought large-scale migration from southern China. Victoria’s 1855 landing tax led many travellers to disembark at Robe in South Australia and walk hundreds of kilometres to the Victorian diggings. Camps, stores and protection stations formed across the goldfields.

1857Violence and survival
Buckland River

An anti-Chinese attack drives a community away

On 4 July, a violent crowd expelled Chinese miners from the Buckland valley, destroying property and causing deaths during the flight. The story belongs within the wider history of racial violence—and of Chinese miners rebuilding lives after it.

1856–62Petition and resistance
Collective action

Chinese Victorians challenge discriminatory taxes

Thousands signed petitions against racially targeted laws. In 1859, many Chinese miners refused the annual residence tax in a widespread civil-disobedience campaign. Their resistance complicates any history that treats Chinese people only as passive victims.

1860s–90sMaking community
Temples, gardens and associations

Life moves beyond the goldfields

Chinese Victorians worked as merchants, market gardeners, herbalists, furniture makers, timber workers, cooks and shopkeepers. Temples, district societies, cemeteries and Little Bourke Street connected people across Victoria and across oceans.

1901–73Restriction and endurance
White Australia era

Families and institutions persist

The Immigration Restriction Act constrained new settlement and family reunion. Yet missions, political groups, newspapers, cafés, families and district associations sustained Chinese Victorian life through decades of exclusion.

1973–todayRenewal and recognition
A changing Victoria

Older histories meet new migrations

Later migration from mainland China, Hong Kong, Taiwan and Chinese diasporas across Asia and beyond transformed the community. Heritage protection, museums, festivals and scholarship made older places newly visible while living culture continued to change.

Chinese Victoria today

Many languages. Many journeys. One evolving state.

Ancestry, birthplace and language are different measures. The figures shown here overlap and should never be used as interchangeable labels for a diverse community.

6.6%of the Victorian population reported Chinese ancestry.
